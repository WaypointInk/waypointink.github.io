Writing with ADHD, and the tools I’m building to keep up

I write three novels side by side. Keeping them straight takes more than willpower.

By Jessica Sellars

I have ADHD. For most of my life that has meant a brain full of ideas and a hard time staying in one place long enough to finish them. Right now I’m writing three long novels side by side, plus picture books. That is a lot of characters, timelines and small details to hold onto.

So I’m working on it, on purpose. I’m actively building ways to strengthen my memory and support how my brain works, instead of fighting it.

The hard part: consistency

When I step away from a novel for a few weeks, details slip. A character’s eye color changes. A town moves three miles down the road. A promise made in chapter four never gets kept. None of that is a creativity problem. It’s a memory problem, and memory problems can be supported.

What I’m trying

  • Writing things down outside my head. Notes on characters, places and timelines live somewhere I can look them up, so I don’t have to remember them.
  • Leaving a note for future me. At the end of a writing session, a sentence or two about where I stopped and what comes next makes it much easier to start again.
  • Short, focused bursts. A clear goal and a set amount of time work better for me than an open-ended afternoon. (This is a big reason I’m excited about the Reedsy Novel Sprint.)
  • Building my own tools. I’ve started making planner tools designed around how my brain works, because the ones I found never quite fit.
  • Consistency passes. After a draft, I use AI to help check for continuity slips, like a name spelled two ways or a timeline that doesn’t add up. It checks my work. It never writes it.

The part no tool replaces

Real people will always read my books before you do. Beta readers and testers catch the things that matter most: where a story drags, where a character doesn’t ring true, where a page didn’t land the way I hoped. I wouldn’t publish without them.
